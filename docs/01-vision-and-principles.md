# Vision and design principles

[← Blueprint index](../README.md)

## Product statement

GRAIL is a mobile strategy game about building a career as a watch dealer. The player finds watches, understands what makes them valuable, negotiates with memorable dealers, commits limited Cash, and chooses how and when to sell.

The game turns watch dealing into a legible strategy loop. Better judgment produces profit. Profit creates buying power. Meaningful activity earns Reputation. Reputation reveals new locations, dealers, and watch opportunities.

The long-term fantasy is a rise from an unknown rookie in a New York bedroom to a connected dealer with access to increasingly desirable markets. The surroundings and stakes grow, but every step is grounded in the same commercial question: **what is this watch worth to me, to the person across the table, and to the next buyer?**

## Player promise

The game should make the player feel:

- **Perceptive** when a visible fact weakens a seller's claim.
- **Decisive** when they accept a strong opportunity or walk away from a bad one.
- **Tense** when Cash and inventory space are committed.
- **Clever** when a profitable exit validates the original judgment.
- **Ambitious** when Reputation reveals a better room, dealer, or market.
- **Connected** when the same characters return with recognizable personalities and specialties.

The player is not simply collecting watch images. They are operating a small business under constraints.

## Design principles

### 1. Deals are the game

Every supporting system should create, clarify, or complicate a deal. Progression should open better opportunities. Characters should change negotiation texture. Monetization should expand access and pace. UI should help the player understand a commercial choice.

If a feature does not make dealing more interesting, it probably does not belong in the MVP.

### 2. Knowledge must become playable

Watch facts are not flavor text. Condition, completeness, and configuration should affect:

- Whether an asking price is attractive.
- Which rebuttal is correct during a negotiation.
- How strongly a dealer values a watch.
- Which marketplace offers match it.
- The resale range the player can reasonably expect.

The game must show the facts needed to make a decision. It should never require hidden real-world expertise to identify the intended answer.

### 3. The player controls the pace

There is no energy system and no daily action allowance. A Game Day changes available opportunities, but does not tell the player to stop playing.

The product supports both quick check-ins and longer sessions:

- A short session may collect a Cash grant, review refreshed stock, or respond to an offer.
- A medium session may negotiate one watch or join an auction.
- A long session may compare dealers, manage inventory, and execute several deals.

### 4. Scarcity creates decisions

The MVP uses a small set of understandable constraints:

- Limited Cash.
- Six starting inventory slots.
- Dealer patience during negotiation.
- Two free auction entries per Game Day.
- Three free active marketplace offer slots.

These limits should create tradeoffs without turning the game into a waiting room.

### 5. Progress is permanent

Reputation does not decrease in the MVP. Opened locations remain available. The player can revisit earlier markets at any time.

This makes experimentation safer and progression easier to understand. A poor deal may cost Cash, but it does not erase career progress.

### 6. Monetization expands opportunity

Gold can open locations, add capacity, buy extra auction entries, and expedite selected processes. Gold never purchases a watch and never converts directly to Cash.

The player may pay for broader or faster access, but still has to judge the deal and fund the watch through the Cash economy.

### 7. Mobile actions should feel tactile and forgiving

The preferred interaction is a press rather than a swipe. The negotiation mechanic therefore uses a long press that fills a pressure meter.

The game may create tension through timing and visible consequences, but it should not demand narrow precision that overwhelms the strategy.

## Experience tone

GRAIL should feel knowledgeable, ambitious, and slightly competitive. Luxury is present, but the player begins far from it. The visual and narrative contrast between the starting bedroom and later locations should make progress tangible.

Dealer dialogue should be concise and confident. It should communicate market personality without requiring the player to understand expert jargon. Watches should feel tactile and desirable, while information remains clear enough to support a decision at a glance.

## What this vision excludes

The MVP is not:

- A passive watch collection gallery.
- A tap-heavy inspection simulator.
- A trivia game based on obscure external knowledge.
- A game where real-money currency directly buys the best watches.
- A punishing career simulation with Reputation loss and permanent setbacks.
- An energy-gated game that ends a session after a fixed number of actions.

## Success test

The product direction is working when a player can describe a memorable deal in plain language:

> “The dealer wanted too much, but I noticed the watch was incomplete. I used that fact, pushed him once more before he lost patience, bought it with just enough Cash left, and later found a buyer looking for that exact configuration.”

That story contains the watch, the person, the pressure, the resource decision, and the exit. That is GRAIL.
